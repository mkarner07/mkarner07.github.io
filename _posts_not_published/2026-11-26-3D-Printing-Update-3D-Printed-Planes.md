As of the time of writing this article, I have printed 350 objects on my 3D printer (and most likely 500+ plates). So since [April]({% post_url 2026-04-23-What-I-Learned-After-200-Prints %}) I printed an additional 150 parts. While that's still a lot, my pace has come down a bit.

But the prints I do have changed.

I do more custom prints (including 3D modelling) for family and friends. While the simplicity of loading up STL files to the printer is great and very convenient, constructing models yourself gives you even more of a "hands-on" feeling. I enjoy it a lot to think of a problem deeply, come up with a solution, construct it in CAD (I use Autodesk Fusion) and then finally print the real physical thing.

In summer, I started to work on more complex models that also consist of electronic components. And that's even more fun than just creating physical objects. It truly gives one the feeling of creating a full product.

A real fun project I did was an [automatic water gun](https://makerworld.com/en/models/1475321-splattershoot#profileId-1539616). It was a highlight of our family summer vacation (not just for the kids).

So after the automatic water gun, I wanted to do more projects in the direction of "full product". I wanted to make something radio controlled. Within the Bambu Lab ecosystem (for reference: I have a Bambu Lab A1 printer) there exists a modular electronics system called "CyberBrick" that you can use to create radio controlled models (cars, boats, forklifts, ...). The physical structure is printed with a 3D printer and the electronics are supplied via these kits. They are a low-barrier entry point for creating radio controlled models.

So, I thought: should I go for an RC car?

There are dozens of models available and I couldn't really decide which one to go for. Then I stumbled on an RC plane. That sounded even more interesting: [GASB ONE](https://makerworld.com/en/models/2202921-gasb-one-world-s-most-printed-rc-plane-x-bambu#profileId-2393635). It is the most 3D-printed RC plane in the world. But it would be a rather complex project and the electronic components involved would be rather costly (> 200 EUR including the controller). That was a bit much for testing the waters to see if this kind of project would be my cup of tea.

I looked further and found a simple 2-channel model called [Hummel](https://makerworld.com/en/models/3039136-micro-aircraft-hummel-rc#profileId-3417235) (the German word for bumblebee). It was a simple two-plate build with some assembly. There even was a list of materials linked on AliExpress totaling about 35 EUR (a bit more with customs). So I thought, let's give it a try. I ordered the components and printed the plates. About 2 weeks later everything was ready to be assembled.

It worked very well and in about half an hour Hummel was ready for its maiden flight. And it flew like a breeze. I didn't think that such a simple 3D-printed plane would fly that well, but it did. I do have some experience in flying RC planes (in my youth I had a simple 3-channel RC motor glider), but I hadn't flown an RC plane for years. So, I basically had to build the skills from scratch. But the great thing with a 3D-printed RC plane is: if it ever crashes, you simply print a spare part.

And I needed that. I didn't have a major crash, but I'd say some rough landings. Therefore, it came in really handy to print a spare within minutes.

I really enjoyed flying Hummel. I enjoyed it even more than I had imagined. So soon I wanted to build another one. From the same creator there is the model [Motte](https://makerworld.com/en/models/3097574-micro-aircraft-motte-rc) (German for moth). It is a similar 2-channel micro-plane with the same electronics but a double-decker structure. I printed it and moved the electronic components from Hummel to Motte (I would have ordered additional components from AliExpress, but unfortunately at that time the receivers were out of stock).

Motte was even more fun than Hummel. It is more agile and not as unstable in slightly windy conditions, but it doesn't fly as fast. However, it is even capable of doing loops. With a charged battery the flying time is about 7 minutes, so with two batteries it's about 15 minutes in total. It's a great activity to clear the head and have a bit of fun.

Right now I am in the process of building GASB ONE. This kind of project is truly my cup of tea and I enjoy it a lot. With the Black Friday sales coming up, I'll order the electronic components and will hopefully be able to fly it in the next couple of weeks.
